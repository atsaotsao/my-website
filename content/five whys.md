 I’ve heard about the “Five Whys” ever since I was in high school. The mental framework seems so simple to comprehend, the goal is to go through the iterative loops of using whys in order to determine the root cause of a problem or idea. It’s extremely effective in exploring the cause-and-effect relationships underlying most problems, but only until you’ve asked enough “Whys” in your life do you realize how much of a mastery this requires.

I always viewed “Five Whys” as putting additionally microscopic lense. You start with the big picture, then you zoom in on a layer, then another, and so on. As someone who’s good at big picture thinking, this makes sense to me because it reminds me to put more effort into zooming on the details, but it wasn’t until I’ve intuitively understood how different perspectives & framing looked like did I realize this was a false assumption.

The truth about the “Five Whys” is so that you can simultaneously do a zoom-in & zoom-out the act; like constantly switching from a microscopic view to an overhead drone shot. The video that best illustrates the scope of that is the 1977 video [Power of Ten](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0fKBhvDjuy0&t=19s). In the video, we learn about how just by increase and decreasing the ten to the power of x we can see a completely different perspective. Each increase or decrease of the power of ten should be the purpose of each whys.

Five whys are such a simple concept but it’s so much harder than you think to master. I’ve also noticed how by meditating every day, I’ve gained more focus, clarity, and patience to practice the art of it every day and apply it across the most important areas of growth.

If you’re still reading this, here’s a reminder to pick something today and ask five whys before you answer it. Don’t ask me why.
