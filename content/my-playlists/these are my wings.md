I avoided listening to hip-hop and rap for more than half of my life. My only exposure growing up was Eminem’s album [_The Eminem Show_](https://open.spotify.com/album/2cWBwpqMsDJC1ZUwz813lo?si=WDndR1xKRmWrQieOTAKgOQ&utm_source=25on25.beehiiv.com&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=these-are-my-wings) (a birthday gift) and a few songs from his next album, [_Encore_](https://open.spotify.com/album/1kTlYbs28MXw7hwO0NLYif?si=pSM9pSdURpOa5Lv359roNQ&utm_source=25on25.beehiiv.com&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=these-are-my-wings).

I used to attribute this lack of exposure to my upbringing, where my musical tastes were largely influenced by my mom teaching her students to play the piano, the songs my older brother would listen to, and the ubiquitous melancholic Taiwanese pop anthems like this timeless [Jay Chou gem](https://open.spotify.com/track/5pIcwtJYNJx93l420oR2Vm?si=e47d912fa23e4fc1&utm_source=25on25.beehiiv.com&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=these-are-my-wings).

But as I got older, I realized there was resistance to the genre and sounds not only because of unfamiliarity but also a deep misunderstanding and unknowing rooted in an underlying internalized racism.

After embarking on an inner-transformation journey, I realized that one’s openness to music genres can mirror how open we are to different perspectives, beliefs, and worldviews.

A few years ago, I finally took the time to sit down and listen to Kendrick Lamar’s [_To Pimp a Butterfly_](https://open.spotify.com/album/7ycBtnsMtyVbbwTfJwRjSP?si=u9iPWuvESKeLDPbOj0pArw&utm_source=25on25.beehiiv.com&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=these-are-my-wings) front to back without interruption, and it **blew** my world wide open.

I realized how much richness of sounds, stories, and poetic expression exists in this huge ocean of music that I hadn’t been paying attention to.

I was wrong and naive to think the majority of hip-hop was mostly about violence or self-aggrandizing around money, power, and fame.

Since then, I’ve become a student of this art form, studying the genre's history and understanding its impact on culture and the world around us.

I’ve learned that the kind of hip-hop that speaks to my soul teaches us not only Black stories and experiences, but also how it relates powerful lessons around spirituality, revolutionary or liberatory movements, and what it means to heal from deep layers of systemic constraints and powers beyond our control.

These sounds are not just beautiful but empowering, providing roadmaps of hope to navigate the tumultuous times we may find ourselves in today.

This month, I’ve chosen some of my favorites across decades, genres, and a range of fusions with jazz, lo-fi, and even funk to showcase the range of this beautiful genre that I feel grateful for.

This is the hip-hop mixtape I would make for my younger self. I hope you enjoy it!
