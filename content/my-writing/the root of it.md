I’ve been having more time to think about my art practice and how it mirrors and responds to the systems I grew up in, the survival strategies me, my family, and my ancestors adopted and what was left stuck, unmoved, unprocessed, suppressed, compressed, and ignored. 

I’ve spent a lifetime examining what happens when whats left behind start to metastasize, clutter, clog, and even rot. And what the flying fantastic F to do about all of it. 

Ever since I landed back in Taiwan, Ive been spending a lot of time with Banyan trees. Admiring them up close, caressing their long beards, sitting against their trunk, and thanking them for the shade they provide.

There was imageries of two specific trees thats been lingering in my head. Both wrapped in yellow caution tape. The first I encountered walking through Sun Yet Sun Memorial Park after dinner, where I saw this beauty. The water beads on the aerial roots glistening from the light from the street lamp. Somehow reminded of me of seeing videos of the rain room exhibit at MOMA the first year I moved to nyc. And I learned that the yellow tape is there because the tree was infected with brown root rot (褐根病). 

The second tree, or whats left of the tree, was right across the street from where I lived. This one was already chopped down and I could see the remaining traces of the fused multi-trunk in this stump. The warning sign said: “The original tree was infected with brown root rot. The decayed roots must be removed, and after the soil is fumigated and sterilized, a new tree will be planted here at a later date."

I cried thinkng about them both. Maybe because I no longer see them as separate to me, but rather ecological mirrors to me, the environment, and world I live in. I feel grief. I see and feel grief of what is, everywhere. 

I see the way it shows up in the kitchen cabinets of my family home. I hear it in the dinner table conversations of our weekly extended family gathering. I feel it in invisible collective field at the training camp, and here at the fire department. I still feel it in my body even after years of therapy, resting, healing, playing, and being.

The day I said to myself before I dared say to others that I wanted to be an artist was the day I made a commitment to myself and my ancestors, that I am willing to go through the depths of shadows, walk through layers & layers of muck to get to the bottom of it; to the root of it all. 

I can never imagine how taking on this path led to forming deeper and more intimate relationships with others exploring their own parallel paths. Oh what a joy. 

I have this vision today of us gathering all together under an ancient banyan tree one day to gather, grieve, laugh, sing, dance, and celebrate the miracle that led us to be with all of it together. 

And if you’re still reading this, and feel even just a tickle of resonance in reading this, always know that you’re never alone wherever you are in the journey.

And there is always a spot for you under the banyan tree.
